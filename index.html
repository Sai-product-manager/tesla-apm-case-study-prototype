01 / Opportunity

The problem to explore

A hypothesis-led opportunity, not a claim about measured Tesla customer behaviour.

When plans change, fixed schedules may not

An EV owner may schedule cabin pre-conditioning for a planned departure. If a meeting runs late or plans change, climate control could run earlier or longer than needed. Separately, owners may have limited visibility into how charging time choices interact with their electricity tariff or available home-energy signals.

This creates an opportunity to test a more adaptive experience: let the driver choose a departure window, decide whether calendar context may be used, and review charging suggestions before anything changes.

Target users

Primary: EV owners with predictable weekday commutes and home charging.

Secondary: Solar and home-battery households interested in shifting flexible charging.

ConvenienceDriver controlEnergy awarenessPrivacy
1
Core job to be done

Help me prepare my vehicle for the time I actually plan to leave, while keeping me in control of comfort and charging choices.

2
Main friction points to validate

Outdated departure assumptions and unclear trade-offs between charging time, cost, and energy availability.

1
Product principle

Suggest first. Ask permission. Never silently override a driver's settings.

DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Control stays with the driver

  • Calendar access is opt-in and can be disconnected at any time.
  • Show the proposed departure adjustment before applying it.
  • Driver can override or stop pre-conditioning immediately.
  • Charging recommendations are advisory; no charging schedule is changed automatically.
  • Use only the minimum information needed for a departure estimate.
Prototype boundary: the switches change this page's simulated UI state only. No personal data is collected or transmitted by this demo.
03 / Product strategy

From hypothesis to MVP

The first release should validate user value and trust—not attempt a broad energy orchestration platform.

Product requirements — MVP

Objective: test whether user-approved departure context can make pre-conditioning easier to manage.

PriorityRequirementAcceptance criteria
MustManual departure windowUser can set, edit, and clear a preferred departure time.
MustPre-conditioning controlUser can start, stop, and override the target temperature at any time.
MustConsent-based calendar contextCalendar connection is off by default; permission and disconnect controls are clear.
ShouldChange-of-plan promptIf an opted-in departure estimate changes, show a prompt before changing a schedule.
ShouldEnergy-aware suggestionDisplay an estimated lower-cost charging window only when tariff or energy data is available and permissioned.
Won'tAutonomous grid dispatchOut of scope for the first MVP; no vehicle-to-grid control or automatic export.

Privacy, consent, and safety requirements

  • Calendar integration is optional, explicit, revocable, and off by default.
  • Prefer extracting a departure-relevant time window; avoid retaining event titles, attendee names, or locations unless a validated use case requires them.
  • Explain what is processed, where it is processed, and how long it is retained. Do not claim “zero-knowledge” or on-device-only processing until the architecture is verified.
  • Do not activate climate control or alter charging without a user-approved rule and a visible override.
  • Respect vehicle battery, thermal, connectivity, and manufacturer safety limits; fail safely when data is unavailable.
  • Use secure authentication, least-privilege permissions, deletion controls, and clear consent logs in any real implementation.

Key assumptions and risks to validate

North-star and guardrail metrics

North-star: percentage of opted-in users who complete a planned departure with the vehicle at their chosen comfort target.

  • Activation: eligible users who enable the feature.
  • Suggestion acceptance: accepted prompts / prompts shown.
  • Departure readiness: departures meeting user-selected target.
  • Energy per pre-conditioning session, measured against a comparable baseline.
  • Trust guardrails: opt-out rate, manual overrides, complaints, and permission revocations.

Targets should be set only after baseline research and a pilot; no performance targets are presented as achieved results here.

05 / Execution

Proposed 12-week validation roadmap

Indicative sequence for a hypothetical cross-functional team. Timing depends on technical feasibility, approvals, and access to test participants.

Weeks 1–2

Discovery

  • Interview EV owners across commuter and home-energy segments.
  • Map current scheduling behaviour and pain points.
  • Review privacy, vehicle, and integration feasibility.
Exit:

Validated problem statement and prioritised use case.

Weeks 3–4

Prototype & test

  • Test departure setup, consent, prompts, and override.
  • Run moderated usability sessions.
  • Refine MVP requirements and measurement plan.
Exit:

Usability findings and agreed MVP scope.

Weeks 5–8

Controlled pilot

  • Launch only after security and safety review.
  • Recruit a small opt-in pilot cohort.
  • Monitor readiness, overrides, opt-outs, and incidents.
Exit:

Reliable pilot data and documented issues.

Weeks 9–10

Evaluate

Compare outcomes against baseline and a suitable control where feasible. Review user feedback, energy measurement quality, trust, and operational burden.

Weeks 11–12

Decision

Make a go / iterate / stop recommendation based on evidence, safety, feasibility, and user value. Do not expand until guardrails are met.
